# Disagreement or Low Forecasts? Challenging Machine Forecast Disagreement Claims

Speaker notes for each slide of [`slides.pdf`](slides.pdf) / [`slides.html`](slides.html).

## 1. Disagreement or Low Forecasts?

One-line claim: the paper finds that stocks on which 100 machine-learning “investors” disagree most underperform by about 1% a month. I rebuilt their measure at full scale and ask a simple question: is this disagreement, or just low forecasts? Most of MFD’s predictive power comes from the investors’ average forecast, which the paper never controls for. Once that average is held fixed, little is left. High MFD sits in the stocks the forests already expect to do worst, and there even the more bullish forests are not optimistic, so the evidence doesn’t show Miller-style disagreement.

## 2. Does investor disagreement predict returns, measured for every stock?

The question: does disagreement among investors predict returns, and can it be measured for every stock without analyst forecasts? The theory is Miller 1977: when shorting is costly, pessimists sit out, the price reflects the optimists, so stocks with more disagreement are overpriced and earn less later. Their contribution is the measurement: 100 machine-learning investors, each seeing different information, and the spread of their forecasts.

## 3. Machine Forecast Disagreement (MFD)

Walk the chain left to right. Each investor is a random forest that sees 76 of the 153 characteristics. Every month each investor forecasts each stock’s next-month return, so each stock gets 100 forecasts. The paper summarises them one way: their standard deviation, MFD, then sorts into deciles, long low MFD, short high. The inputs come from the Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen dataset: 153 characteristics that earlier papers found predict returns, computed the same way for every US stock each month from CRSP and Compustat. Full detail is in the backup slide on the data.

## 4. Same mean forecast, different disagreement

Two stocks in the same month, December 2019, picked to have the same mean forecast, +0.94% per month. Each histogram is the 100 investors’ forecasts. Stock A is in the lowest MFD decile: the investors agree, standard deviation 0.06%. Stock B is in the highest MFD decile: same average, but the forecasts spread from 0.5% to 1.3%, standard deviation 0.15%. The paper’s claim is that stocks like B are overpriced and later underperform. Keep in mind that here the mean is held fixed by construction; in the full cross-section, MFD and the mean are strongly negatively correlated.

## 5. Low-MFD stocks beat high-MFD stocks by about 1% a month

Left: next-month value-weighted return by MFD decile in our replication. Returns fall from about 0.9% a month for the lowest-disagreement decile to slightly negative for the highest. Right: the long-low, short-high strategy earns about 1% a month over the paper’s sample (the paper reports a 1.14% spread, t 4.3), though it has been flat since about 2008 and lost money after the paper’s sample. Now the concept of controls: a high-MFD stock might just be small, volatile or a past loser, all of which already predict low returns. A control holds those known predictors fixed and asks whether MFD still adds information. The paper does this three ways: factor-model alphas, Fama-MacBeth regressions with 12 characteristics, and a mechanism test where the effect is concentrated in stocks that are expensive to short. The result survives all of them. Note that every control is a characteristic or a factor; keep that in mind for later.

## 6. Is MFD a new signal, or the mean forecast in disguise?

I started by studying the effect itself, and in my rebuild the spread is there: about 1% a month between the lowest and highest MFD deciles. A natural question followed. MFD is one summary of the 100 forecasts, their spread. The same 100 forecasts have another summary, their average: the forests’ own expected-return forecast for the stock. So how orthogonal is MFD to that mean? The first check says not very: the cross-sectional correlation is about −0.5, so high-MFD stocks are also stocks the forests expect to do badly. That gives the real question: does MFD add any signal beyond the mean forecast? The paper never asks, because none of its controls is the mean; they are all characteristics and factors. Side note if asked: this also matters in practice, since anyone averaging an ensemble of return models gets the spread for free. I originally planned an extension splitting disagreement into optimists and pessimists, but this check came first and changed the talk.

## 7. MFD predicts returns only because it tracks the forests’ own mean forecast. Hold that mean fixed and MFD has no predictive power.

This is the specific, testable objection. Not general skepticism: a single regression that either kills MFD or doesn’t. Disagreement and the mean forecast are both summaries of the same 100 forecasts. Under the paper’s story, disagreement carries information beyond the average view. Under mine, it carries none once the average is known. I fixed five tests and their pass/fail criteria before running any of them; the table is in appendix A4. Test 1 is a gate, the rebuild; the rest each have a result that would rescue the paper. Miller’s mechanism test, whether the optimist side drives the effect, is in A2.

## 8. Next-month returns across MFD decile portfolios

The gate passes. I reran the forests at the paper’s full scale. Same returns and sort for both bars, only the MFD signal differs. High-minus-low is −1.02% a month value-weighted against the authors’ −1.09%, and −1.33% vs −1.32% equal-weighted, with matching six-factor alphas. The alpha is the intercept from regressing the decile 10 minus decile 1 return each month on the five Fama-French factors (market, size, value, profitability, investment) plus momentum: the part of the spread those known factors can’t explain. So whatever I find next is about their measure, not a broken rebuild.

## 9. Add mean forecast, and MFD’s slope goes to zero

Two regressions, run the paper’s way: each month, regress next-month returns across stocks, then average the slopes over 556 months. Numbers sit under the term they belong to, t-statistics in brackets. Regression 1 is the paper’s: return on MFD plus their 12 controls. The MFD slope b is −0.196 with t −5.5, close to their −0.23, t −5.4. Regression 2 adds one variable, the mean of the same 100 forecasts. Now b is −0.006 with t −0.13: indistinguishable from zero. The mean forecast itself has slope 0.47 and t 9. I ran regression 2 on the authors’ published MFD file too, to rule out anything in my rebuild: −0.02, t −0.4. MFD has no information about returns beyond what the average forecast already contains. All variables are standardised each month, so slopes are % per month per 1 SD.

## 10. Hold the mean fixed and MFD’s alpha vanishes on average

All portfolios here are equal-weighted. Left panel: sort on the mean forecast first, then on MFD within each column. The bottom row is the six-factor alpha of high minus low MFD. It is zero in four of five columns, and the average is −0.06, t −0.4. The lowest-mean column is the exception, −0.79, t −4.3, but within it the high-MFD stocks have a mean forecast of −27 basis points versus −1 for low MFD, so the column still varies a lot in the mean: that is the mean’s tail, not disagreement. And with value weights, the MFD high-minus-low alpha is not significant in any mean-forecast quintile, including the lowest, so even that exception lives in small stocks. Right panel: sort on MFD first, then the mean forecast. Its alpha is large and significant in every MFD column, +1.28 on average, t 7.9. One more thing worth noting: the mean’s spread grows with MFD, from 0.85 to 1.73. That is the one place disagreement matters, as an interaction.

## 11. The paper’s other evidence fails the same way

The paper backs MFD with robustness checks: more controls, and longer return horizons. Both rest on MFD alone, so I reran each with the mean forecast added. Left: five control sets, from none, to size, book-to-market, momentum and reversal (not the same as the paper’s 4-control spec), to the paper’s 12, up to all 153 characteristics plus industry dummies, MFD alone has t between −5.4 and −7.0. Add the mean and every t falls inside the not-significant band. The weakest case is the richest set, t −1.9, about a third of the original slope and at the edge of significance; adding mean squared as well gives the same picture. Right: a defender could say overpricing corrects slowly, so MFD should matter at longer horizons even if the mean absorbs it at one month. It does not. MFD alone fades from −0.20 to about −0.05 over a year. With the mean, its slope is near zero at every horizon from 1 to 12 months.

## 12. Swap the forests for ridge: same result

Is this something peculiar to random forests? I rebuilt the 100 investors as ridge regressions, each on exactly the same feature subset as its forest counterpart, and computed ridge MFD and the ridge investors’ own mean forecast. Each model is only compared with its own mean. With the paper’s basic controls, ridge MFD is significant on its own, −0.16 with t −4.7; adding the ridge investors’ mean takes it to −0.06, t −1.7, not significant. The forest is the main result again: −0.33 to −0.02. If asked about the full 12 controls: forest is −0.20 to −0.01; ridge MFD is already insignificant on its own (−0.02, t −0.9), so there is nothing left for its mean to absorb. Whatever model generates the investors, their disagreement carries no information beyond their average forecast.

## 13. High-MFD stocks are the textbook anomaly short leg

I’ve shown empirically that MFD is a proxy for the mean forecast. Now the economics: which stocks do the forests disagree about? Take the highest MFD quintile and rank it on the paper’s own characteristics, where 50 is the typical stock. High-MFD stocks are far more volatile, more lottery-like, higher beta, more illiquid, faster-growing in assets, smaller and much less profitable. That is the textbook short leg of the best-known anomalies: idiosyncratic volatility, MAX, profitability and investment. Why would forests disagree about exactly these stocks? A random forest predicts by averaging returns of similar stocks in its training data. For an ordinary stock there are thousands of neighbours, so whichever 76 features a forest drew, the answer is similar. For an extreme stock there are few, so the feature draw decides the forecast. So MFD is high because the forecast is low. It is the mean forecast in another form, not a disagreement premium.

## 14. What survives in the lowest mean forecast quantile is mean and dispersion, not Miller’s disagreement

Part 3 left one corner: the lowest mean-forecast quintile, where MFD still earns −0.79 a month equal-weighted, t −4.3. Is that disagreement? Three checks. First, the quintile is wide: high-MFD stocks there have mean forecasts about 26 basis points lower than low-MFD stocks, so the sort hasn’t really held the mean fixed. Splitting the quintile into 2, 4 or 5 finer bins shrinks that gap to about 4 basis points, and the equal-weighted alpha falls to about −0.5, t around −2.5. The picture, left to right: the same lowest-forecast 20% cut into 1, 2, 4 and 5 slices; each grey box is one slice, side by side; in each, the dumbbell is the gap between the average mean forecast of high-MFD stocks (orange) and low-MFD stocks (blue), and we compare high minus low within each slice. As the slices get finer the dumbbells shrink, from 26 to 4 basis points. Every dumbbell starts from the same baseline, so only the length carries information, and it is to scale. These are gaps in the forests’ forecast, not in realized returns: realized, the corner’s high-MFD stocks earn −0.83% against +0.28% for low-MFD, a 1.1% spread. Forecasts are compressed, so a 26 bp forecast gap is large in forecast terms. Say the alphas aloud, averaged across slices: equal-weighted −0.79, −0.63, −0.48, −0.60 (t −4.3, −3.3, −2.3, −2.9); value-weighted −0.15, −0.32, −0.25, −0.12. Value-weighted it is insignificant at every bin width, so what’s left lives in small stocks, and size, illiquidity and volatility controls absorb it: in a regression inside the quintile with the paper’s 12 controls, MFD is −0.19, t −1.9, alone and zero with the mean. Second, why the forests disagree here at all: these are the extreme stocks from the last slide. With few similar stocks to learn from, which features a forest drew decides its forecast, so the spread is mechanical, not a difference of opinion. Third, Miller needs optimists who bid the price above fair value. For high-MFD stocks in this corner, the most bullish forests, the 80th percentile of the 100, forecast about +0.07% a month, against +0.54% for the typical stock. For 99% of these stocks even the optimists expect less than a typical stock, so if the forests were investors nobody would hold these stocks at this price. There is no optimist here to overprice anything, so this is not disagreement in Miller’s sense. Put together: the spread that survives is not investors disagreeing, it is models that cannot learn these stocks well from so few similar ones. MFD measures how hard a stock is to model. If pushed that MFD only needs to rank disagreement, not match its level: the optimist-versus-pessimist split in A2 tests that, and it fails too. The heatmap of where disagreement sits across all stocks is A3. 1976–2022, FF6 alphas, Newey-West t.

## 15. MFD’s spread is mostly the forests’ own low forecast. For a typical stock, model dispersion adds nothing.

How this challenges the paper: they show MFD survives 12 controls, but every control is a characteristic or a factor, and none is the forests’ own mean forecast. Add it and MFD’s slope goes from t −5.5 to t −0.1, and the optimist side, which Miller says should drive the effect, doesn’t. The 1% spread is real, but it is mostly the forests’ own expected-return forecast. Hold that forecast fixed and MFD predicts nothing for about 80% of stocks, at every horizon and under every control set, in my rebuild and in the authors’ own file. What survives is in the lowest-forecast fifth: small, extreme stocks, where the equal-weighted residual disappears with standard controls, and where even the most bullish forests expect less than a typical stock. So it doesn’t look like Miller’s disagreement; it looks like dispersion from models that can’t learn these stocks well. What I can’t rule out: these hard-to-model stocks are also hard for investors to value, so model dispersion there may partly track real disagreement. Separating the two needs a measure not built from the same characteristics, such as lending fees or analyst dispersion, which is the next step. For production: don’t trade the dispersion on its own; for a typical stock it adds nothing to the mean forecast. Use it to condition the mean: the mean sorts returns much more sharply when MFD is high, 2.00% versus 0.79% a month. High dispersion tells you a stock is hard to predict, and that is where the mean’s ranking matters most, so downweighting those stocks would throw away the best predictions.

## 17. Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen stock characteristics

If asked about the data: everything comes from the Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen dataset, the one from their 2023 Journal of Finance paper on the replication crisis. It computes 153 characteristics that published papers found predict returns, the same way for every stock each month, from CRSP and Compustat. Six groups follow Hou, Xue and Zhang's categories; the "new" group is JKP's own additions. After the paper's filters there are about 2,700 stocks a month. About 13% of values are missing, mostly long-lag return seasonality and R&D; the paper fills them with the month's median and ranks every characteristic into minus one to one, so the forests only see relative positions. The 100 investors each draw 76 of these 153.

## 18. Miller says the optimists drive the effect. They don’t.

Miller’s mechanism, if asked. Miller’s theory is one-sided: pessimists who can’t short stay out, so overpricing comes from how far the bulls sit above the typical view. So I split MFD into the optimist side and the pessimist side. They correlate 0.59, so the data can tell them apart. Without the mean, both sides are negative and their difference is insignificant: a symmetric effect, more like uncertainty than Miller. With the mean, both are zero. Fama-MacBeth, 1976–2022, z-scored, % per month per 1 SD. The other half of Miller, that the effect is strongest where shorting is costly, I can’t test properly without fee data. Short interest is a weak proxy and gives the opposite of Miller’s prediction, so I mention it only if asked.

## 19. Forests disagree most where they expect the worst returns

If asked how tightly disagreement and the mean forecast are linked. Each month, sort stocks into quintiles by the forests’ mean forecast, then ask how each quintile splits across MFD quintiles. Each column sums to 100%, and if MFD and the mean were unrelated every cell would be 20%. Instead there is a clear diagonal. Look at the top row, the highest-disagreement stocks: almost half, 49%, of the lowest-forecast quintile is in it, against only 8% of the highest-forecast quintile. Disagreement is concentrated exactly where the forests expect the worst returns. And they are right about these stocks: the lowest quintile loses about 0.1% the next month, the highest earns 1.3%. Across all top-MFD stocks, the share from the lowest-forecast quintile is 62%. Equal-weighted, 1976–2022, monthly shares averaged over time.

## 20. Five tests, each with a result that would rescue the paper

If asked how the tests were chosen. I wrote these criteria before running the tests. Test 1 is a gate: if I cannot reproduce their spread, I cannot claim anything about it. Test 2 is the key regression. Test 3 does the same thing without regressions, by sorting. Test 4 reruns the paper’s robustness checks, more controls and longer horizons, with the mean added. Test 5 asks whether this is specific to random forests, by rebuilding the investors as ridge regressions. Miller’s mechanism test, whether the optimist side drives the effect, is in A2.
